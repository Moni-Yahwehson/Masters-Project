## Causative agent

Lumpy skin disease (LSD) is caused by lumpy skin disease virus (LSDV), a
double-stranded DNA virus in the genus *Capripoxvirus*, family *Poxviridae*.
The genus also contains sheeppox virus and goatpox virus. The three viruses are
closely related and are not distinguishable by routine serological tests, which
is why serology alone cannot separate LSD from sheep and goat pox in mixed
flocks and herds.

LSDV is a large, enveloped, environmentally stable virus. It survives for long
periods in scabs and necrotic skin material shed from lesions, and it persists
in contaminated premises, equipment and vehicles. This environmental stability
is a major reason cleaning and disinfection are part of every control programme.

## Susceptible hosts

Cattle (*Bos taurus* and *Bos indicus*) and water buffalo (*Bubalus bubalis*)
are the natural hosts. *Bos taurus* breeds, and in particular thin-skinned,
high-producing European dairy breeds, generally develop more severe disease than
indigenous *Bos indicus* breeds. Young calves, lactating cows and animals in
poor body condition typically show the most severe clinical signs.

The disease does not infect humans. LSD is not a zoonosis, and meat and milk
from recovered animals do not present a human health risk, although milk yield
losses and trade restrictions cause serious economic damage.

## Morbidity, mortality and economic impact

Morbidity in an affected herd typically ranges from a few per cent up to about
45 per cent, and is strongly influenced by breed, immune status and vector
abundance. Mortality is usually low — commonly under 10 per cent — but the
economic impact is disproportionate to the death rate. Losses arise from:

- sharp and often permanent drops in milk yield;
- abortion and temporary or permanent infertility in bulls;
- hide damage from scarring, which downgrades leather value;
- emaciation and secondary bacterial infection of ulcerated nodules;
- movement restrictions, trade bans and the cost of vaccination campaigns.

## Distribution and seasonality

LSD was historically confined to sub-Saharan Africa. Since the 2010s it has
spread through the Middle East, south-eastern Europe, the Russian Federation,
central and south Asia, and into east and south-east Asia. Because the disease
is spread mainly by biting arthropods, incidence is strongly seasonal: outbreaks
peak in warm, wet periods when vector populations are highest, and decline in
cold or dry seasons. Outbreaks frequently follow animal movement along trade
routes, river valleys and irrigation schemes.

Any suspicion of LSD in a previously free country or zone is an emergency. LSD
is a WOAH-listed disease and suspicion must be reported to the competent
veterinary authority.
