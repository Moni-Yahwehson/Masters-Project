## There is no specific antiviral treatment

Management of a clinical case is supportive. Recommended measures:

- Isolate affected animals from the rest of the herd and from vectors where
  possible (housing, screened pens, or at minimum separate grazing).
- Clean ulcerated nodules and apply topical antiseptic or fly repellent to
  prevent myiasis and secondary bacterial infection.
- Use broad-spectrum antibiotics **only** to treat secondary bacterial infection
  — pneumonia, mastitis, infected ulcers — not against the virus itself.
- Provide non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs under veterinary direction for
  fever and pain, and fluid or nutritional support to animals that stop eating.
- Give soft, palatable feed and easy access to clean water for animals with oral
  lesions.
- Observe all withdrawal periods for milk and meat when medicines are used.

Recovery in uncomplicated cases takes several weeks; emaciation, scarring and
depressed milk yield may persist far longer.

## Vaccination

Vaccination is the single most effective control measure and the backbone of
every successful national programme.

- **Homologous live attenuated LSDV vaccines** (Neethling-type strains) give the
  best protection and are generally preferred.
- **Heterologous vaccines** based on attenuated sheeppox or goatpox strains are
  used in some regions but may confer weaker or shorter protection in cattle;
  their use may also be constrained where sheep and goat pox is absent.
- Coverage matters more than product choice. Effective control requires at least
  around 80 per cent coverage of the target cattle population — patchy
  vaccination leaves enough susceptible animals for transmission to continue.
- Vaccinate the whole at-risk population, not just around known cases, and
  revaccinate annually before the vector season.
- Mild post-vaccination reactions occur: local swelling at the injection site
  and, occasionally, small generalised nodules ("Neethling response"). A
  differentiating PCR distinguishes vaccine-strain from field-strain lesions.
- Use a fresh needle per animal. Reused needles spread the virus mechanically.

## Outbreak control measures

- **Immediate notification** of suspicion to the competent veterinary authority.
  LSD is a WOAH-listed notifiable disease; reporting is a legal obligation in
  most jurisdictions, not a discretionary step.
- **Movement standstill** on affected and at-risk holdings, and control of
  animal movement, markets and gatherings in the surrounding zone.
- **Ring or mass vaccination** around outbreaks, following national policy.
- **Vector control**: insecticides and repellents on animals, larval habitat
  reduction, removal of standing water and manure management.
- **Cleaning and disinfection** of premises, vehicles and equipment. LSDV is
  susceptible to detergents and lipid solvents, sodium hypochlorite, phenolic
  compounds, iodine compounds and quaternary ammonium disinfectants, but is very
  stable in dried scabs, so mechanical removal of organic material must come
  first.
- **Safe disposal** of carcasses, scabs, bedding and contaminated material.
- **Quarantine** — commonly 28 days — for introduced animals, with clinical
  inspection before mixing.
- **Stamping-out** of affected and in-contact animals is applied in some
  countries pursuing eradication in a previously free zone; policy varies and is
  set by the national authority, not by the herd owner.

## What a farmer should do on suspicion

1. Isolate the affected animal or animals immediately.
2. Contact the local veterinary officer or animal health authority the same day.
3. Stop all movement of cattle on and off the holding until advised.
4. Do not sell, slaughter or move suspect animals.
5. Start vector control and use a fresh needle per animal for any treatment.
6. Keep records of which animals are affected and when signs appeared.
