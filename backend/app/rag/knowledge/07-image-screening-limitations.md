## What a photograph cannot show

An image-based screening tool observes only surface morphology under whatever
lighting and framing the photographer used. It cannot observe:

- rectal temperature and the febrile course;
- lesion depth on palpation — the single most useful sign distinguishing the
  full-thickness LSD nodule from superficial mimics such as pseudo-lumpy skin
  disease and dermatophilosis;
- superficial lymph node enlargement;
- milk yield, appetite, respiratory signs and mucosal lesions;
- herd-level context: morbidity, movement history, vaccination status, season
  and vector pressure.

Because these are exactly the features that separate LSD from its differentials,
an image-derived result is a **triage signal**, not a diagnosis.

## What a binary CNN classifier can and cannot do

A convolutional classifier trained on two labelled folders of cattle images
learns to separate those two folders. Consequences that must be stated in any
report generated by such a system:

- **Closed-world assumption.** A model trained only on healthy versus LSD-affected
  cattle has no class for ringworm, dermatophilosis, mange, insect bites or
  photosensitisation. Presented with any of those, it must still emit one of the
  two labels it knows. A high probability therefore means "more similar to the
  LSD training images than to the healthy training images", not "LSD".
- **Calibration is not accuracy.** A sigmoid output is not a calibrated
  posterior probability of disease unless it has been explicitly calibrated
  against a representative validation set with known prevalence.
- **Distribution shift.** Performance drops on breeds, coat colours, lighting,
  camera angles and backgrounds that differ from the training set.
- **Validation must be stratified.** If a validation split does not contain both
  classes in representative proportion, the resulting accuracy, precision and
  recall figures are not interpretable. A split that accidentally contains a
  single class produces metrics that look confident and mean nothing.
- **Prevalence matters.** In a low-prevalence population, even a specific
  classifier yields many false positives, so positive predictive value falls.

## How results should be communicated

- Report the model's probability together with the decision threshold used, so
  the reader can see how close to the boundary a case sits.
- Treat probabilities near the threshold as **inconclusive** rather than forcing
  a label.
- Present differential diagnoses alongside any positive result.
- Always direct the user to a veterinarian for physical examination and to the
  competent authority for notification and laboratory confirmation.
- Never present an image-derived result as a confirmed diagnosis, and never use
  it as the basis for culling, treatment or trade decisions on its own.

## Appropriate role in a control programme

Used correctly, image-based screening helps where veterinary coverage is thin:
it can prompt earlier reporting, help extension workers prioritise farm visits,
and support surveillance dashboards. Its value comes from shortening the time to
a professional examination, not from replacing one.
