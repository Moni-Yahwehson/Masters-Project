## Vector-borne mechanical transmission

The dominant transmission route is **mechanical** transfer by blood-feeding
arthropods. There is no evidence that LSDV replicates in the vector; the insect
simply carries virus on contaminated mouthparts from an infected to a naive
animal. Implicated vectors include:

- stable flies (*Stomoxys calcitrans*), considered a highly efficient vector;
- horse flies (Tabanidae);
- mosquitoes, particularly *Aedes aegypti*;
- hard ticks (*Rhipicephalus* and *Amblyomma* species), which may also transmit
  transstadially and transovarially.

Because transmission is vector-driven, outbreak risk tracks vector abundance:
warm, humid weather, standing water, irrigation, and high stocking density all
increase risk. This is why LSD spreads in waves during the wet season and slows
markedly in cold or dry conditions.

## Direct and indirect transmission

Direct contact transmission is comparatively inefficient but does occur. Virus
is present in:

- skin nodules, scabs and crusts, in high concentration and for long periods —
  scabs are a major source of environmental contamination;
- saliva, nasal and ocular discharge;
- milk;
- semen, which can remain positive for weeks after clinical recovery, making
  natural service and artificial insemination a genuine transmission route.

Indirect spread occurs through contaminated needles, surgical and ear-tagging
equipment, vehicles, feed and bedding. Reusing a needle across animals during a
vaccination or treatment round is a well-documented iatrogenic route.

## Shedding timeline

Viraemia is generally short, on the order of one to two weeks, and roughly
coincides with the febrile and early nodular phase. Virus persists in the skin
lesions considerably longer than in blood — commonly up to about 5 weeks, and
detectable in scabs for months. Animals are therefore infectious to vectors
mainly while lesions are active, but contaminated material stays hazardous long
after clinical recovery.

Subclinically infected animals occur and can be viraemic without visible
nodules. They are epidemiologically important because they move undetected.

## Key risk factors for introduction and spread

- Introduction of animals from an affected area without quarantine or testing.
- Communal grazing, shared watering points and livestock markets.
- Uncontrolled animal movement along trade and transhumance routes.
- High vector density and absence of vector control.
- Low or patchy vaccination coverage in an at-risk population.
- Thin-skinned high-yielding dairy breeds in a naive population.
