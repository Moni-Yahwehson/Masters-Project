## Incubation and early signs

Under field conditions the incubation period is generally 1 to 4 weeks; after
experimental inoculation, nodules appear roughly 4 to 7 days later. The WOAH
Terrestrial Animal Health Code uses an incubation period of 28 days for
regulatory purposes.

The first signs are usually non-specific and precede the skin lesions by about
two to five days:

- fever, often 40–41.5 °C, which may be biphasic;
- marked drop in milk yield;
- depression, reluctance to move, and loss of appetite;
- excessive salivation, and ocular and nasal discharge;
- pronounced enlargement of the superficial lymph nodes, especially the
  prescapular and precrural nodes.

## Characteristic skin nodules

The defining lesion is a firm, raised, round, well-circumscribed skin nodule
approximately 1–5 cm in diameter. Key morphological features used in the field
case definition are:

- **Depth.** Nodules involve the full thickness of the skin, the subcutis and
  sometimes the underlying muscle. They feel hard and deeply seated rather than
  superficial and scurfy.
- **Distribution.** Lesions are typically multiple and **generalised** over the
  whole body. Common sites are the head, neck, muzzle, nostrils, perineum,
  udder, scrotum, vulva and limbs. Generalised distribution is one of the
  strongest discriminators from localised skin conditions.
- **Evolution.** Nodules may develop a central area of necrosis. The necrotic
  plug — the "sit-fast" — separates to leave a deep, full-thickness ulcer that
  discharges and later granulates. Healing leaves a permanent hairless scar that
  damages the hide.
- **Number.** Cases range from a handful of nodules to hundreds covering the
  animal. A small number of lesions does not exclude LSD.

## Mucosal and internal lesions

Nodules are not confined to the skin. Ulcerative lesions occur on the mucous
membranes of the mouth, nose, pharynx, trachea, oesophagus, rumen, abomasum and
the reproductive tract. Nasal and tracheal lesions cause discharge, noisy
breathing and, in severe cases, respiratory obstruction and death. Ocular
lesions can cause conjunctivitis, corneal opacity and blindness.

## Other systemic consequences

- Painful oedematous swelling of the limbs, brisket and udder, causing lameness
  and reluctance to stand.
- Mastitis and teat lesions, which make milking painful and predispose to
  secondary bacterial infection.
- Abortion in pregnant cows; orchitis and prolonged or permanent infertility in
  bulls, with virus shed in semen for weeks.
- Emaciation that can persist for months after the acute phase.

## Practical field case definition

A suspect LSD case is a bovine or water buffalo showing fever with multiple
firm, deeply seated, generalised skin nodules 1–5 cm across, with enlarged
superficial lymph nodes, in an area or season where the disease is plausible.
Clinical suspicion is sufficient to trigger notification and sampling; it is
never sufficient for final confirmation, which requires laboratory testing.
